# Stonetop GM Moves

Pick and land a GM move at the table: recognize when one is owed, choose soft or hard, and shape it to the fiction already established.

## Your agenda

Everything said or done as GM serves exactly three goals — nothing else:

- **Portray a rich and mysterious world.** Ground it in mundane detail (crops, weather, neighbors' opinions) and let the fantastic stay strange, scary, and poorly understood.
- **Punctuate the characters' lives with adventure.** The PCs have homes, families, and demands on their time — the job is to interrupt that life with threats and opportunities only they can address, not to let adventure become their whole existence.
- **Play to find out what happens.** Make plans and prep, but once play starts, follow where the players, the dice, and the fiction lead. Don't steer toward a story decided in advance.

If a move under consideration doesn't serve one of these three, don't make it.

## Your principles

Guidelines for pursuing the agenda. They'll sometimes conflict — use judgment:

- **Follow the rules.** Give moves their full effect; don't fudge results because they feel too easy or too hard. Never call for a move before its trigger is met in the fiction.
- **Begin and end with the fiction.** Fiction → move → fiction, always. Ground every roll in a concrete action first; describe what the result looks like after.
- **Address the characters, not the players.** Ask "what do you do," not "what does your character do." Keeps everyone thinking and answering in-fiction.
- **Ask questions and build on the answers.** Constantly. Treat answers as true from then on, even the surprising ones.
- **Be a fan of the player characters.** Root for them, but don't shield them — threaten what they care about, give them hard choices, and let them earn their wins.
- **Embrace the fantastic and the mundane.** Contrast them deliberately; each makes the other feel more real.
- **Exploit the setting guide.** Use it as a springboard, not scripture — change specific details freely, be cautious about changing broad themes.
- **Respect your prep.** Once a prep decision is made (an NPC's motive, a monster's instinct), treat it as settled fictional truth during play.
- **Give your characters life.** Name NPCs, give them wants and quirks and a memorable trait; extend the same care to beasts and monsters.
- **Think offscreen, too.** Consider what NPCs and factions are doing off-camera, and what the PCs' actions cause off-camera.
- **Bring it home.** Tie scenes back to Stonetop and the people in it — who sees them off, who worries, who resents their return.
- **Let things breathe.** Let time pass in a sentence; don't chase every hour. Let distant threats simmer before zooming in.
- **Let things burn.** Protect no NPC, no plan, no darling out of attachment. Threaten what matters and follow through on failure.

## The core loop

The underlying shape of every session:

1. **Establish the situation** — frame the action, describe the environment, ask questions, portray NPCs, answer questions.
2. **Make a soft GM move** — provoke action and/or raise tension.
3. **Ask "What do you do?"**
4. **Resolve their action** — run the triggered player move, or make a hard move on a miss/ignored trouble, or just say what happens.
5. **Repeat** — back to step 3 if the situation's clear and actionable; back to step 2 if it needs to escalate or needs a nudge; back to step 1 if it's unclear or the scene's over.

A GM move is what fills steps 2 and 4. This skill is about choosing and executing that move well; for how to run the loop, frame scenes, and manage the spotlight overall, see the source chapter's "Running the Game" material — this skill focuses on the moves themselves.

## When a move is owed

Make a **soft move** any time the situation is clear and it's the GM's turn to push things along — this happens constantly, as a routine part of the loop, not just in emergencies.

Make a **hard move** when:

- a player rolls a **6-** on a triggered move (unless the move itself says otherwise), or
- a PC **ignores an already-established threat or trouble** entirely.

Also reach for a move (soft-shaped, chosen to fit the specific instruction) when a player move's result explicitly asks for one — e.g., a 7-9 on Defy Danger calling for "a lesser success, a cost, or a consequence." Pick whichever GM move produces that specific outcome in the fiction: using up a resource as a cost, putting someone in a spot as a consequence, demonstrating a downside as a lesser success.

Don't ration moves or wait for a "big" moment. Soft moves are the ordinary texture of play; hard moves should land the instant they're triggered, not get saved up.

## Soft moves vs. hard moves

A **soft move** provokes or foreshadows — it gives the PCs a beat to react before the bad thing fully lands. "The torches are getting low." "It hisses and coils, about to strike — what do you do?" Soft moves can also just crank tension without demanding an immediate response.

A **hard move** establishes badness *now*, with no chance to head it off — only to deal with the aftermath. It should make the situation significantly worse: an action fails outright, something is lost, an injury lands, the danger closes the distance before anyone can act. A 6- doesn't always mean the triggering action fails — it means something bad happens, possibly unrelated to the action itself, possibly even alongside a success.

Calibrate severity to the fiction, not to a fixed scale — a hard move against a PC who was warned and pressed on anyway can be brutal; a hard move in a lower-stakes moment can be barely worse than a soft one would've been. What makes it "hard" is the lack of an out, not the raw damage number.

Never announce the move by name. Say what happens in the world. "Rocks fall, everyone take 1d10" is a mechanic wearing a move's name; "the ceiling's been groaning since the tremor, and now a slab of it lets go right over Vahid's head" is a move.

## The moves at a glance

| Move | What it does |
|---|---|
| Announce trouble (future or offscreen) | Signal that something bad is coming, or already happening elsewhere, before it can be acted on directly. |
| Reveal an unwelcome truth | State a fact the PCs wish weren't true — it may have been true all along, or become true just now. |
| Ask a provocative question | Put a question to a PC (often through an NPC) that forces a decision, reaction, or piece of fiction only they can supply. |
| Put someone in a spot | A sharper version of announcing trouble — someone must act right now or it gets worse. |
| Use up their resources | Whittle away something they have — concrete (torch, shield), abstract (supplies), intangible (trust, reputation), or purely mechanical (HP, Loyalty) — always shown in the fiction. |
| Turn their move back on them | Flip the thing they were trying to do so it works against them instead. |
| Demonstrate a downside | Show the real-world cost of a choice, a playbook, or a piece of gear — crude weapons against iron, warm armor in the sun, being small or old or young. |
| Hurt someone | Inflict a specific, painful, consequential wound — more than a HP number — on a PC or an NPC they care about. |
| Separate them | Split the party, put an obstacle between them, or take someone out of the immediate scene. |
| Capture someone | Take a character prisoner — softly (still in-scene, escapable) or hard (cut away, already gone). |
| Offer an opportunity (with or without a cost) | Hand over an opening — sometimes free, sometimes at a stated or implied price. Often made just by asking "what do you do?" without first threatening anything. |
| Tell them the consequences/requirements (then ask) | Name what a course of action will cost or require before they commit, then let them decide. |
| Advance towards impending doom | Bring one of a threat's or hazard's grim portents to pass — almost always delivered *through* one of the other moves above, not as a move of its own. |

These are the general-purpose moves, usable in any scene. There are separate move lists for expeditions and site exploration, for threats already in play (see `stonetop-threats`), for time spent at home, and for specific dangers or NPCs — reach for those when that element is actually active in the scene.

The full text, tier examples, and licensing-safe paraphrases of the book's worked dialogue for every move above are in `references/moves-reference.md`.

## Choosing the move that fits

Start from what's already established — the situation, the fiction, and any prep — and pick the move that follows naturally from it. A 6- while tracking something through the woods might reasonably reveal that the party is now lost; it should never reach for something disconnected just because it'd be a bigger jolt.

Let the specifics of the PC and their action shape the move. The same 6- against a charging beast looks different for a Heavy who braced a spear to meet it than for a Fox who tried to dodge past and stab it on the way by — the badness should grow out of *how* they failed, not a generic template.

When nothing obvious presents itself, don't force it — just say the plainly bad thing that follows. The list exists to unstick a GM who's drawing a blank, not to be checked off.

## Reading the tone

Stonetop is grounded hearth fantasy, not grimdark. A move landing well usually means:

- **Consequences ripple into relationships, resources, and standing at least as often as they land on HP.** A lost bit of Loyalty, a spent set of supplies, or a favor now owed can carry as much weight as damage.
- **Contrast the fantastic against the mundane.** A supernatural threat plays better against a backdrop of harvest worries and neighborly gossip; a mundane problem benefits from a fantastic detail dropped in.
- **NPCs can be hit harder than PCs.** A follower losing a limb shows the stakes are real without deprotagonizing a PC — but don't ambush an animal companion with brutality; players get protective of those.
- **Warn before anything permanent.** Only inflict a disabling, irreversible injury on a PC if the danger was clearly telegraphed and they either missed or knowingly pressed on anyway. If they weren't warned, keep the hard move recoverable.
- **A strong hit (10+) still gets a move where appropriate.** A player rolling well doesn't pause the fiction — the rest of the scene (other foes, the environment, an NPC's reaction) keeps moving unless a player explicitly seizes the initiative.
