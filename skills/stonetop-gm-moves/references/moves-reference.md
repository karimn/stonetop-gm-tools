# GM Moves — full reference

Detailed guidance and paraphrased worked examples for each of the thirteen general-purpose GM moves. See `SKILL.md` for the agenda, principles, core loop, and the at-a-glance table. Examples below are paraphrased in original wording from the scenarios in Stonetop Book I's "Running the Game" chapter — consult the source (linked in `SKILL.md`) for the book's actual dialogue.

## Announce trouble (future or offscreen)

The most versatile move — trouble almost always provokes a reaction or at least raises tension. As a hard move, it can flip a peaceful scene into a fight, or make clear that something the PCs did will have consequences later.

- *Soft:* A commotion at someone's door in the middle of the night — knocking, a friend calling out, alarm bells in the distance — pulls a PC out of sleep and into a situation that's clearly already underway.
- *Hard:* A scarred, armed stranger watches two PCs leave a tavern, then quietly signals a companion to follow them out.
- *Soft, low-key:* An NPC who's been pushed too far storms off in a huff — nothing happens right now, but it's clear there'll be a reckoning later.

## Reveal an unwelcome truth

Establish something as true that the PCs wish weren't — whether it was true all along and is only now surfacing, or the GM is deciding it's true in this moment. Great for resolving Know Things or Seek Insight: on a 7+ the answer must be honest, even if it isn't pleasant; on a 6- the screws can be turned further.

- An informant who's asked about a missing contact claims total ignorance, all exaggerated innocence — while very obviously fishing for a bribe or a deal in return for saying more.
- Investigating a disturbed clearing turns up churned mud, drag marks, and blood on the underbrush — evidence a companion was ambushed, laid out plainly rather than withheld.
- A 6- while searching for a missing person doesn't come back empty — it comes back with a body, found half a mile off, clearly killed by something with a piercing attack.

## Ask a provocative question

Ask a question — directly, or through an NPC — that forces a decision or an emotional response, even if the response is a choice not to act. Alternatively, ask a question that asserts something bad while handing the player the job of authoring the specifics.

- An NPC medic reports that a badly wounded companion probably won't survive and will slow the group down, then flatly asks whether they should "take care of it."
- A PC who witnessed something strange and unsettling from an ally is simply asked, in the moment, what's going through their head as they watch it.
- On a bad roll during a tense conversation, rather than the GM dictating exactly what was said, the player is asked what they said or did that landed so badly.

## Put someone in a spot

A sharper version of announcing trouble: someone has to act right now, or things get worse. The person put in the spot doesn't have to be the one who gets to do something about it — the spotlight can shift to someone else entirely.

- A PC barely catches a falling branch after a failed jump and is left dangling from a dangerous height, grip already slipping, with no help in immediate reach.
- A local tough stands up and confronts a PC directly, and several of his equally large friends start rising from their seats at the same time.
- While one PC calms a dangerous animal, a second drake is spotted sneaking up behind her from an angle she can't see — spotted only by a different PC watching from cover.

## Use up their resources

Something whittles away at what the PCs have, or takes it outright. Resources can be concrete and specific (a torch, a shield), concrete but abstract (supplies, ammunition), intangible (reputation, someone's trust), or purely mechanical (HP, Fortunes, Loyalty) — but a mechanical loss should always be shown landing in the fiction, not just deducted silently.

- Hours underground burn through the last of the torchlight, with only minutes left before the party is in the dark.
- A fall through branches costs both a chunk of supplies (crushed in a pack) and a chunk of HP on the way down.
- An NPC who's been quietly insulted just shuts down emotionally and walks away — whatever Loyalty they were holding is gone.

This move frequently rides along with others — HP loss in particular tends to happen as part of a "hurt someone" or "advance towards impending doom" move, not as a move on its own.

## Turn their move back on them

Especially good on a 6-: take whatever the player was trying to do and flip it so it works against them. Give the advantage to their opposition, make them supply something bad for the GM to use, or turn a move that should expand their options into one that narrows them.

- A character trying to Know Things about a dangerous substance instead surfaces their own past traumatic encounter with it — which the player is asked to describe.
- A miss on Seek Insight still yields an answer to one of the move's questions, but now acting on anything else is actively disadvantaged.
- An attempt to talk someone into betraying an ally instead gets neatly turned around, with the NPC coolly probing for whatever damaging information the PC might let slip instead.

## Demonstrate a downside

Show the limits of a move, a playbook, or a piece of gear in the fiction — a crude weapon failing against iron, warm armor failing in blazing heat, the specific disadvantages of being small, tall, young, or old.

- A heavy pack keeps making noise at exactly the wrong moment, and the noise doesn't go unnoticed by whatever's nearby.
- A child character trying to speak up in an adult conversation gets talked over and dismissed outright.
- A solid hit against a massive creature ends with the weapon itself failing — snapped, stuck, or otherwise unusable — even as the blow lands.

## Hurt someone

Inflict a specific, consequential wound — more than a bare HP number — on a PC or an NPC they care about. It always means damage; against a PC it may also mean a debility, and it always means a problematic wound that becomes part of the ongoing fiction.

Scale the wound to the fiction: the source of the harm, whether it followed a 7-9 or a 6-, the damage rolled, and any tags on it (messy, forceful). A clean hit from a minor foe reads very differently from a monstrous 6- with heavy damage. Only inflict a permanent, disabling injury if the danger was clearly telegraphed beforehand and the PC either missed a roll against it or knowingly pressed on anyway — never spring a maiming on a PC with no warning. NPCs, by contrast, can be hurt more brutally and without the same warning requirement, which is a good way to demonstrate real danger without deprotagonizing a PC — with one exception: don't casually brutalize an animal companion, since players tend to be protective of them.

- A stab wound to the leg is described first in plain terms, then modulated by the damage rolled — a small number reads as a stagger and a burning ache that still holds weight; a large number reads as the leg giving out entirely in a spray of blood.
- A creature with obviously massive, tree-crushing jaws was explicitly called out as capable of taking a limb before a PC chose to stand and fight it anyway — a 7-9 against it can plausibly cost the arm, because the warning was given and heeded-but-ignored.
- The same kind of creature, presented as merely dangerous rather than explicitly limb-taking, instead costs a large damage roll and a debility on a 7-9 — painful and serious, but recoverable, because the maiming warning was never given.

## Separate them

Split the party. Put a foe or obstacle between PCs. Have someone fall behind, go missing, or get physically removed from a fight. Pairs especially well with "put someone in a spot" — being alone in a spot is worse than merely being in one.

- Pushing through dense terrain, the group gradually spreads out until one PC realizes they can no longer see (and soon can't hear) anyone else.
- A difficult conversation ends with the other party in tears, turning and shutting a door, leaving the PC on the wrong side of it, alone with what they just did.
- A magical ward drops without warning between two PCs mid-exploration, physically cutting the party in two with no immediate way through.

## Capture someone

On the soft version, capture the character but stay in the scene — leaving room to escape, or for allies to attempt a rescue. On the hard version, cut straight past the capture itself to the aftermath: already bound, already being dragged off, with the details of exactly how skipped over.

- A hostage-taker signals an accomplice, who grabs a PC and puts a blade to their throat mid-conversation, then starts backing toward an exit with the PC still in the room.
- A PC touches something clearly otherworldly and is found sometime later collapsed beside it, unresponsive, with no memory of what happened in between.
- Two PCs investigating in the dark are simply gone by the time the scene cuts back to them — a torch found guttering on the ground is the only sign anything happened.

## Offer an opportunity (with or without a cost)

Hand over an opening — specific ("act now and you'll catch them by surprise") or general ("they haven't noticed you, what do you do?") — with a definite cost, a possible cost, or none at all. This move often happens without the GM even framing it as one: handing the initiative to a player with a plain "what do you do?" during a tense but unthreatened moment is already offering an opportunity.

- A blow that doesn't quite finish an armored foe still visibly staggers them, leaving them off-balance for whatever comes next.
- Bait that hasn't worked yet might land if a PC visibly and believably left camp, drawing attention away from the others left behind.
- A dangerous-looking object in the environment is pointed out as something that could be smashed to cause a useful distraction, at an obvious risk.

## Tell them the consequences/requirements (then ask)

Use this as an interrupt the moment a player declares an intention, to clarify stakes before committing them to a roll. Use it also to open a path forward when one seemed blocked, provided they're willing to pay some cost — time, resources, an opportunity foregone.

- Leaving a fallen companion's body behind will unsettle a superstitious crew, but properly disposing of it will cost time and might give away the party's position — the choice is put to the player before either happens.
- Taking a drastic action right now means forgoing any chance to get clear of what follows — confirmed before it's locked in.
- A closer, harder look is needed to make sense of a puzzle beyond what's possible in the field — but that kind of study needs a controlled setting and reference material the character doesn't have on hand right now.

## Advance towards impending doom

Threats and hazards can carry a series of grim portents leading toward an impending doom. This move decides that one such portent has now come to pass and shows it in the fiction. In practice this almost never stands alone — it's delivered *through* one of the other moves (announcing trouble, revealing an unwelcome truth, putting someone in a spot), dressed as that portent coming true.

Rarely, a GM might mark the portent as having happened offscreen without revealing it yet — that's legitimate, but only if there's a genuine commitment to it having occurred; otherwise no move has actually been made.

- Finding a missing child safe, but surrounded by disturbing ritual imagery and speaking with unsettling cheer about "her drawings," reveals that whatever's been quietly working on her has progressed further than anyone realized.
- A structurally unstable ruin, already groaning from a fight, finally starts coming down after one more heavy impact — dust and cracking stone signaling the portent of collapse has arrived.
- A vivid, symbolic dream that ends in something violently breaking free coincides, on waking, with a very real earthquake already underway — the portent and the reality landing in the same beat.
