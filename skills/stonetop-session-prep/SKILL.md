# Stonetop session prep

Prep is what happens between sessions: reviewing notes, deciding what might come up, and doing the thinking you don't want to do live at the table. This skill covers what to prep and how to shape an adventure out of a threat or opportunity. For the GM's live-table move list (the moves you actually make once play starts), see the `stonetop-gm-moves` skill instead.

## Why prep, and how much

Prep exists to serve play, not to replace it. It gives the GM interesting things to say, speeds up table time (no stopping to stat a monster or draw a map from scratch), shores up known weak spots (if followers always get forgotten, prep something for them to do), and — importantly — gives permission to play hard. A GM who's written "tear an arm off" into a monster's move list beforehand is much more likely to actually do it than one improvising in the moment.

Two different kinds of prep exist, and it matters which one is being done:

- **Possibility prep** — things the PCs *might* run into: a danger, a discovery, a complication. This stays in a quantum state. It isn't true until it actually gets used in play.
- **Binding prep** — real decisions about how the world is: a monster's instinct and HP, who actually killed the widow, what happens if nobody intervenes. This is fictional truth already, whether or not the players know it yet. Make these decisions carefully, then respect them at the table — don't fudge them later because the story "should" go a different way.

Prep only as much as is useful. Don't write down what's obvious or memorable without notes. Never prep a plot, a character arc, or a scripted outcome — that's deciding what happens in advance, which is the opposite of the game's whole approach. Prep situations, people, and places; leave the events to the table.

## What to prep between sessions

| Category | What it covers | Where to go deeper |
|---|---|---|
| Threats | Recurring problems that get worse if unchecked — instinct, impending doom, grim portents | `stonetop-threats` skill |
| Hooks | The specific way a threat or opportunity gets dropped in front of the PCs | This skill, below |
| Expeditions | Chart a Course choices, a route, points of interest, rough encounters | Expeditions chapter (not yet in this plugin) |
| Sites | Any important place the PCs will explore — what it's like, its story, a map if useful | Not yet in this plugin |
| Dangers | Hazards, monsters, hostile NPCs they might run into | `stonetop-threats` / `stonetop-harm` skills |
| Discoveries | Puzzles, clues, treasures, arcana | Not yet in this plugin |
| NPCs | Names, defining traits, an instinct, connections — full stats only if they might become a follower | `stonetop-threats` skill (for NPCs that are also threats) |

Not every category needs attention every session. Prep what will actually get used: the sites, dangers, and NPCs the PCs are likely to hit given where the fiction currently stands.

## Choosing the adventure's seed: opportunity or threat

Each time an adventure needs a starting point, the campaign's Seasons Change roll (made at the end of introductions, and again each time the town moves through a season) tells which kind of seed to build around:

- **10+ → an opportunity.** The players chose interesting news, valuable insight, or a trade opportunity. Build something external (it needs to pull the PCs out of town) but nearby (reachable and returnable within a few days). Tie it to a steading improvement, a goal a player has voiced, or a threat already on the books that this news could finally crack open.
- **7-9 → a threat, flavored by a gain.** A threat surfaces or worsens; the players also picked a seasonal gain (interesting news, valuable insight, unexpected bounty, trade opportunity, Tor's Blessing, or population boom). Build the threat first — external, nearby, Stonetop's own problem, and urgent — then let the chosen gain shape *how* it's revealed. A trade opportunity means a neighboring trader stumbles into it or profits from warning the town; unexpected bounty means the threat is discovered by whoever went out to collect the bounty; population boom ties it to a newcomer or a batch of newly-adult youths, and so on.
- **6- → threats abound.** Start from the worst threat already established (or invent one), give it the same external/nearby/urgent/Stonetop's-problem shape, and let it have already done real damage before the session starts. Keep a couple of secondary threats simmering in the background for texture, without developing them fully yet. This is likely to trigger *Meet with Disaster* (Fortunes down).

This isn't a one-time, first-session-only decision — it's the mechanism for seeding *any* adventure. Whenever the campaign needs its next adventure, ask what the last Seasons Change (or equivalent turning point) produced, and build from there the same way.

Once the seed is chosen: write up the threat (full write-up if it has momentum; at minimum a type and instinct — see `stonetop-threats`), and think hard about *why now*. What changed that turned a background threat into today's problem? Tying that answer back to something the players already established makes the adventure feel earned rather than arbitrary.

## Shaping the adventure

An adventure — first or fiftieth — tends to move through the same beats:

1. **Setup** — loaded questions (and maybe a love letter) that establish facts, spotlight relevant setting elements, foreshadow, and make the stakes personal to specific characters.
2. **Set the scene** — zoom into an ordinary moment of daily life at the place and time the hook needs to land.
3. **Drop the hook** — trouble, an unwelcome truth, or an opportunity, introduced through GM moves.
4. **Reactions** — NPCs (especially ones with relationships to the PCs) respond to both the hook and the PCs' likely course of action, before or as the PCs prepare.
5. **Preparations & travel** — Chart a Course, Outfit, Requisition, recruiting followers; then the journey itself, which can become its own adventure.
6. **Resolution** — the PCs actually engage the threat or opportunity at its focal point.
7. **Aftermath** — the trip home, the town's reaction, loose threads that seed the *next* threat or opportunity.

Prep can usefully target stages 1-4 in real detail, since they're where specific facts and NPCs matter most. Stages 5-7 get looser: prep the route and a few likely encounters for travel, prep the site/danger/discovery for the resolution, and deliberately leave the aftermath almost entirely unprepped — it depends entirely on what actually happened.

### Refining the hook

A good hook is:

- **Actionable** — it implies an obvious first move the PCs could take.
- **Inclusive** — it gives every PC, not just one, a reason to react.
- **Urgent** — sitting on it clearly costs something.

Work out who's involved, what exactly happens, and roughly when and where — but stop short of scripting the PCs' response.

### Setup questions

Before framing the first scene, write loaded questions for the players to answer in character. They serve four purposes at once: establishing facts the hook needs to be true, surfacing setting elements the players should already know about, foreshadowing without spelling anything out, and making the situation personally load-bearing for each character. Ask about the past before the present, and cut any question that doesn't clearly serve one of those four purposes. A love letter — a private note asking one player to describe an experience, then rewarding a roll with insight or a resource — is a good way to foreshadow something too on-the-nose to ask about directly.

### Setting the scene and dropping the hook

Work backward from the hook: what time of day, what's happening around town, where do the PCs need to be? Frame that ordinary moment first, ask a few more questions to flesh it out, and use homefront-style moves (introduce someone interesting, reveal simmering tension, oppose a wish, play NPCs against each other) to make the town feel lived-in before trouble arrives. Then make the moves that drop the hook itself, and spend real time on reactions — this is often where the most interesting player-move triggers (Persuade, Seek Insight, Know Things) happen, and where future threats first reveal themselves.

### If the hook doesn't land

Never force the issue. If the players seem checked-out or genuinely don't think the hook is worth the risk, that's information, not a problem to route around with more pressure. Check in out of character if something feels off. Otherwise, shift into loose downtime, keep making moves that show the honest consequences of inaction — advance a grim portent, have an NPC's patience run out, let an opportunity actually get taken by someone else — without treating it as a punishment. Sooner or later a fresh crisis (or the accumulated cost of the old one) will produce the next hook.

## In-play judgment

**Prep situations, not stories.** Never prep a plot, a character arc, or an assumption about how a scene resolves. The moment prep starts answering "and then what happens" instead of "what's true right now," it's become a script — and running a script instead of following the dice and the players is the one thing this game explicitly asks a GM not to do.

**The Seasons Change result is a seed, not a script.** It picks a category (threat or opportunity) and a rough shape. Everything specific — who, why now, how it's revealed — still gets built by asking what's already on the table from prior sessions and reaching for the most resonant answer, not the most obvious one.

**Mundane and fantastic both need airtime.** If the seed is uncanny, prep at least one purely human complication (weather, a worried parent, low supplies) to go with it. If the seed is mundane, prep one strange or magical wrinkle. The contrast is what keeps the world feeling both real and mysterious.

**Leave blanks on purpose.** Not everything needs an answer before the session. A prepped mystery with a deliberately unresolved detail is a hook for a future session — write it down as an open question, not as a placeholder to fill in later.

**Prep is only "spent" once it hits the fiction.** Anything possibility-tier — a danger, an NPC reaction, an encounter — can be re-skinned, dropped, or held for later if the table's actual direction doesn't need it this session. Don't feel obligated to use every idea just because time went into it.

**Between-session review feeds the next prep cycle.** After a session, look back at where prep held up and where it didn't: which moves got missed, where the GM got stuck, what a little more prep would have fixed next time. That review — not a fixed checklist — is what should decide what gets prepped before the next session.
