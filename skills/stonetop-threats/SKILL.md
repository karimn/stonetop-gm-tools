# Stonetop threats

Use this skill to turn a recurring problem — an NPC, a monster, a circumstance — into a threat: a tracked, escalating source of trouble with its own logic for how it acts and where it's headed.

A threat is not every danger the PCs face. A pack of giant spiders blocking a ruin's entrance is just an obstacle if it won't get worse over time. Write something up as a threat only when it's going to matter later — when it has momentum, or when it's clearly going to keep causing trouble unless the PCs deal with it.

Threats are prep, not play. Make the binding decisions about a threat's nature and direction between sessions, not on the fly at the table.

## Threat anatomy

Every threat needs the first three of these; everything else is optional and gets added as it becomes relevant.

| Element | What it is | Required? |
|---|---|---|
| Name & type | A short name plus one of the 8 types below | Yes |
| Tracker placement | Homefront / Nearby / Distant, per its location | Yes |
| Instinct | "To \<do something\>" — the behavior that makes it a problem | Yes |
| Description | Who/what it is, how it became a problem, its ties to other NPCs/threats | Recommended |
| Impending doom | The irrevocable bad outcome if it goes unchecked | If it has momentum |
| Grim portents | 2-4 concrete milestones on the way to the doom | If it has a doom |
| Stakes | Open questions about the threat you're genuinely curious about | Optional |
| GM moves | 2-4 moves defining its specific behavior/capabilities | Optional |
| Player moves | Custom moves triggered by interacting with it | Optional |

Not every field needs filling immediately. A freshly-noticed threat might be just a name, type, and instinct for weeks before it earns a doom or stakes.

## The 8 threat types

Every threat gets exactly one type. The type comes with a menu of GM moves that represent how that kind of threat typically causes trouble — use them anytime the threat is involved, on or off screen, alongside your normal GM moves. When unsure which type fits, skim the move lists; the one that feels most natural to reach for is usually right.

| Type | Is fundamentally... | Instinct example |
|---|---|---|
| Affliction | A behavior, circumstance, or condition — not a person or thing | *to infect and spread* (a plague) |
| Beast | A creature or NPC ruled by appetite/instinct, not plans | *to dominate its territory* (a predator) |
| Institution | A power structure that acts through invested individuals | *to maintain the status quo* (a council) |
| MacGuffin | An object, place, or secret people fight over | *to inspire jealousy* (a coveted artifact) |
| Magical entity | A spirit/Fae/god-thing, bound by strange rules | *to be adored* (a vain Fae) |
| Rabble | A group united by emotion/circumstance, not shared purpose | *to do whatever the hell they want* (bandits) |
| Villain | One ruthless, capable person | *to maintain or increase his power* |
| Wildcard | A mysterious or untrustworthy individual — the catch-all | *to cling to how things "should" be* |

Full move lists, longer type descriptions, and a dozen worked examples (including the book's complete Sajra the Swyn walkthrough) are in `references/threats-reference.md` — pull it up when you need move inspiration or a type is genuinely ambiguous.

## Writing a new threat, step by step

1. **Name & type.** Pick something distinguishable from your other tracked threats.
2. **Add it to a tracker.** Homefront (in town), Nearby (within a couple days' travel), or Distant (further). Note name, a short descriptor, type, and roughly where it is.
3. **Give it an instinct**, if it doesn't have one already (some NPCs/monsters come with one — reuse or adapt it).
4. **Write a quick description.** Only what you'll need reminding of: who/what it is, how it became a problem, and ties to other NPCs, places, or threats. If a threat commands lesser threats, either give those a full write-up of their own or fold them in parenthetically (type + instinct) inside the main description.
5. **If it has momentum, write its impending doom**, then 2-4 grim portents leading to it (see below).
6. **Optionally, write stakes questions** — things about the threat's future you're honestly curious about.
7. **Optionally, pick 2-4 GM moves** from its type (plus monster moves, if applicable) that especially fit this specific threat.
8. **Optionally, write a custom player move** if the threat warrants one (e.g. "When you remove the seal from the tomb, roll +CHA...").

Leave blanks you don't need to fill immediately — either decide now or explicitly leave the question open for later.

## Impending doom and grim portents

The impending doom is the answer to "if nothing stops this, where does it end?" It must be **irrevocable** — something that permanently changes the situation, not something the PCs can simply undo later. It doesn't have to be catastrophic ("Dilwen never forgives Rhianna" is as valid as "the hordes sweep south"). Not every threat needs one; if nothing comes to mind, leave it blank and revisit later.

Grim portents are 2-4 concrete, observable milestones on the way to the doom. Two hard rules:

- **They happen in the world, not to the PCs' plans.** "Townsfolk form a cult" is a portent. "The PCs learn who the cultists are" is not — it presumes investigation and success, which is railroading disguised as prep.
- **They can involve the PCs but never presume an outcome.** "Cultists attack the PCs at home" is fine — you can safely bet they'll be home eventually, and the fight is still open. "Cultists capture the PCs" already decides the fight for them.

**Writing them:** work backward from the doom. Ask "what observable event happens right before this?" — that's your last portent. Repeat until you reach something visible from where things stand right now. Lean on the threat's type-moves and instinct for inspiration at each step; if it's also a statted monster, its monster moves are fair game too. Expect to revise the doom itself as the chain takes shape — a loose outline beats a locked plan.

**Advancing them at the table** is one of your basic GM moves (*advance towards impending doom*): check off the next portent and show the PCs a concrete sign of it. Reach for this when a Seasons Change roll says a threat worsens or threats abound, when a PC rolls a 6- and this is the hard move that fits, or when the PCs return to a place/threat they've been away from and it's time to show the cost of their absence.

## Stakes

A stakes question is something about the threat's future you're genuinely uncertain about and want to be surprised by — not "will this grim portent happen" (you've already decided that), but things like "will Vahid convince Eira to trust him?" or "who will die in the coming battle?" Not every threat needs stakes, and it's fine to add them later once you're curious enough.

Running a stakes question well means deliberately not deciding the answer yourself:

- **Put your stakes on screen.** If you're wondering whether a bond can be broken, have someone show up begging to have it broken.
- **Put it in the PCs' hands** — state the consequences or requirements and let them choose.
- **Play NPCs with integrity** — when the answer hinges on what an NPC would do, decide based on their instinct and history, not on the story you want.
- **Follow the moves and the dice** — when a triggered move can resolve or advance the question, let it.
- **Let it simmer** if it doesn't resolve. Don't force it; come back later.

## Threats across a campaign

Stonetop Book I doesn't use the word "front" — if that term is familiar from other Powered by the Apocalypse games, the closest equivalent here is a threat's write-up plus its place on the trackers. There's no separate multi-threat structure to build; the campaign layer emerges from tracking many threats at once and letting their instincts, portents, and dooms run concurrently and interact. A collection of threats comes alive when they tangle: a rabble subservient to a villain, an institution that could shelter or exploit an affliction, a wildcard whose fate is bound up in a magical entity's portents. When writing a new threat, always ask what it's connected to on the trackers already — those connections are where campaigns generate themselves.

### Between-session threat review

After every session, walk your trackers and update each threat:

- Resolved, dead, or gone? Cross it off.
- Type or instinct no longer fits (you got it wrong, or it's evolved)? Revise it.
- Learned something new? Add it to the description.
- A portent came to pass? Check it off. PCs changed its course? Rewrite the remaining portents/doom, or cross the doom off entirely if it's fully averted.
- Now has momentum where it didn't before? Write it a doom and portents for the first time.
- A stakes question got answered, or stopped mattering? Cross it off; add new ones you're now curious about.
- Its capabilities were revealed or changed in play? Write it new GM moves.
- It moved geographically? Move it between trackers.

Also scan the session itself for new threats: enemies or rivals made, monsters that could return, NPCs the PCs can't fully trust, groups in conflict with the PCs or each other, lingering bad circumstances, or anything the PCs now hold that others want. Anything that fits, write up — even just name, type, and instinct for now.

## In-play judgment

**Don't over-formalize minor threats.** A newly-noticed problem (a rumor, a suspicious NPC) is often just a name + type + instinct for a long while. Full write-ups with dooms and stakes are for threats you already know matter.

**A threat move vs. a hard GM move vs. advancing a portent are different levers, and you'll usually reach for them in that order of severity.** A threat's own moves are your default flavor for "what does this specific villain/beast/institution do right now" during a scene — softer, reactive, tied to what just happened. Advancing a grim portent is a bigger, plot-level beat, reserved for the moments described above (Seasons Change results, a 6- that calls for a hard move, or the PCs returning after time away) — it's not something to do every session out of obligation. If a threat has no doom yet, or its portents are all still ahead of the current scene, just use its type moves and instinct to color the scene instead of reaching for doom advancement.

**Let threats change type and instinct as they're played, without treating that as a mistake.** The source material's own example: an NPC first written as a wildcard ("to nitpick and snipe") got played nastier and more controlling at the table than planned — the GM simply rewrote her instinct ("to undermine others' value") and added stakes questions after the fact, matching the write-up to what actually happened in play rather than forcing play back onto the original write-up.

**Not every threat-shaped situation has one culprit.** When two relatives of missing children were bitter and nasty toward the PCs, the initial instinct was to write them up as a rabble threat — but the better call was recognizing the real threat as an affliction, "distrust of the PCs," with the relatives caught up in it rather than causing it. If a rabble/institution write-up isn't producing useful moves, ask whether the people involved are actually an affliction's symptoms instead.

**Build grim portents backward from the doom, not forward from the present.** Guessing forward from "what happens next" tends to produce a vague escalation. Asking "what would I have to see, right before the doom, to know it's about to happen" and then chaining that question backward reliably produces portents that are concrete and sequenced correctly — this is the technique the book's own Sajra example uses.

**Stakes questions are a deliberate refusal to plan an outcome — protect that.** The temptation is to quietly decide the answer while prepping, then play it out as scripted. The value only shows up if you actually let dice, NPC integrity, and PC choices settle it, including leaving a question open across multiple sessions if it doesn't resolve cleanly.
